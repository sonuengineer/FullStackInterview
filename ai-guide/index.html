How AI Thinks

Kinds of AI, how human language works, what happens inside a model, how real apps use it, and how to build AI you can trust. Every idea is explained in simple words, and almost every box lets you try it yourself.

An AI chat model cuts your words into small pieces, then guesses the next piece again and again until the answer is done.

In machine learning (ML), nobody writes the rules. You give the computer many examples with the right answers, and an algorithm (a method) finds the pattern by itself. Common ML algorithms

Supervised

Learns from examples with answers (this email is spam). Most common type.

Unsupervised

Gets data without answers and finds groups or patterns by itself.

Reinforcement

Learns by trying things and getting rewards or penalties, like training a pet.

Narrow vs general-purpose AI

One job, or many jobs?

Narrow AI is trained for one task only. General-purpose AI (like an LLM) does many different tasks with the same model, just by changing the instructions. Pick a model.

AGI (Artificial General Intelligence) means AI that can do any thinking task as well as a human. Today's LLMs are general-purpose, but most experts say they are not AGI yet, and they disagree about when (or if) it will happen.

Generative AI

Sorting things vs making new things

Classic AI mostly sorts or predicts: spam or not, a price, yes or no. Generative AI creates something new: text, images, sound, video or code. Click each button a few times and watch which output changes.

    Raw LLM vs chat model

    A raw LLM does not answer. It only continues text.

    A raw LLM (also called a base model) comes straight out of training on internet text. It has one skill: continue text in the most likely way. It does not know it should answer you. Extra training turns it into a helpful chat model. Pick a prompt and compare.

    How a raw LLM becomes a chat model

    Step 1

    Pre-training

    The model reads trillions of tokens of text (websites, books, code) and learns to guess the next token.

    Result: raw LLM (base model)
    Step 2

    Instruction tuning

    It trains on many example conversations written by people: a question, then a good answer. Also called SFT.

    Result: it learns to answer, not just continue
    Step 3

    Preference tuning

    People (or other AIs) compare two answers and pick the better one. The model learns to be helpful, honest and safe. Also called RLHF.

    Result: chat model
    AI agents and agentic AI

    From answering to doing

    A chatbot answers, then you do the work. An AI agent is an LLM working in a loop: it thinks, uses a tool, looks at the result, and repeats until the job is done. Agentic AI is the bigger idea: AI systems that plan and act on their own toward a goal, often with several agents working together and asking you only for important choices.

      Human language

      Language is the hardest thing for computers, because people mean much more than the words they say. This is what NLP and LLMs are trying to handle.

      Natural language processing Why language is hard
      Natural Language Processing (NLP)

      Teaching computers to understand language

      NLP is the part of AI that works with human language: reading it, understanding it, translating it and writing it. Older NLP systems did this in separate steps, one tool per step. Pick a sentence and click through the steps. Today one LLM does all of these inside a single model.

      NLP is used for: Translation Spam detection Voice assistants Search engines Grammar checkers Summaries Chatbots
      Why human language is hard

      People understand more than words

      To understand a sentence, people use context, grammar, word order, the speaker's real intention, culture, tone, and what was said earlier. Many words also have several meanings. A computer gets only the text. Pick a topic and try it.

        Tokenizer

        AI reads pieces, not words

        A tokenizer cuts text into small pieces called tokens, and gives each one an ID number. Short common words are one token. Long or rare words are cut into parts. You pay per token, and all limits are counted in tokens.

        0characters
        0words
        0tokens

        A simplified cutter to show the idea. Each AI company has its own tokenizer with its own vocabulary (often 30,000 to over 250,000 tokens). Rule of thumb: 100 tokens is about 75 English words.

        How a tokenizer is built

        Glue the most common pair, again and again

        Most LLM tokenizers are built with BPE (Byte Pair Encoding). Start with single letters. Count which pair of neighbors appears most often in the training text, glue it into one new token, and repeat. Common words become one token; rare words stay in pieces. Press the button.

        Embeddings

        Words become places on a map

        Inside the AI, every token becomes a list of numbers, like map coordinates. Words with similar meaning land close together. That is how AI knows "puppy" is like "dog". Click a word.

        Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of numbers per token, not 2. The same idea powers "search by meaning" in vector databases.

        RNN (Recurrent Neural Network)

        The old way: read one word at a time

        Before transformers, language AI mostly used RNNs. An RNN reads words one by one and keeps a small memory that it rewrites after every word. Old words slowly fade. Read the sentence and watch what happens to "keys".

        RNN memory after this word (8 numbers, darker = bigger)
        How much the memory still holds "keys"0%

        LSTM and GRU are improved RNNs with better memory, but they still read one word at a time. That makes them slow to train on huge amounts of text.

        Transformer

        The new way: read everything at once and pay attention

        The transformer was introduced by Google researchers in 2017 in the paper "Attention Is All You Need". It is the design inside Gemini, Claude, GPT and Llama. It reads all tokens at the same time and uses attention to decide which other words matter for each word. Click a word to see where it looks, then change the last word.

        Query, Key, Value (Q, K, V)

        How attention decides where to look

        Attention works like searching a library. Every word makes three lists of numbers. Its Query is compared with every word's Key; a good match means more attention, and the word then takes in more of that word's Value.

        In real models, Q, K and V come from multiplying each token's embedding by three learned tables of numbers (weight matrices). They have hundreds of numbers each, and many attention "heads" run side by side, each looking for different things like grammar, names or topic.

        Greedy vs sampling, temperature, top-k, top-p

        How the next word is picked

        The model gives every possible next token a chance. Then a picking method chooses one. Greedy always takes the top token, so you get the same answer every time. Sampling rolls a weighted dice, shaped by temperature (how wild), top-k (only the k best words) and top-p (only the best words that add up to p).

        Good to know: some newer models no longer let you set temperature, top-k or top-p at all. They manage sampling themselves and reject these settings, so you shape the answer with other controls instead, such as how much thinking or reasoning effort to spend and how many tokens it may use. The mechanism below is still what is happening inside the model; you just may not be holding the dials.

          How models learn

          Training: guess, measure the mistake, adjust, repeat

          A new model is only random numbers (weights) and knows nothing. Training shows it an example, lets it guess, and measures how wrong the guess was: the loss. Then it works out how much each number caused the mistake (backpropagation) and nudges every number a little in the better direction (gradient descent). One full pass over all the training data is an epoch. Below, a tiny model with just 2 numbers learns how study hours affect test scores.

            Training data

            LLMs train on trillions of tokens: web pages, books, code and conversations. The data is cleaned (duplicates, spam and unsafe text removed). Some is kept aside as test data the model never trains on, to check it learned patterns instead of memorizing (overfitting).

            GPU training

            Training is billions of simple multiplications, repeated over and over. A GPU does thousands of them at the same time. Big models train on thousands of GPUs (or Google TPUs) connected together, running day and night for weeks or months.

            Why it costs so much

            The work grows with model size times amount of data. On top of the final run come failed runs, experiments, electricity, cooling, data preparation and expert salaries. Estimates for the biggest models run from tens to hundreds of millions of US dollars.

            Multimodal AI

            Pictures, sound, PDFs and video become tokens too

            A multimodal model understands more than text. Each kind of input goes through an encoder that turns it into vectors, just like text tokens. Then one transformer reads everything together, so you can ask "what is wrong in this screenshot?" or "summarize this meeting recording". Pick an input type.

            Building an AI app

            User, UI, server, model and tools, checked answer

            A real AI app is more than one model call. The server sits in the middle: it checks the request, loads data, calls the model and tools, checks the answer, and only then replies. Pick a request and press Play, or click any box to see its job and where it lives in a typical chat app.

              Memory + context window

              AI forgets what does not fit

              The AI has no memory. Each time, the app sends the chat again. But the AI can only read a limited amount at once: the context window. Old messages that do not fit are simply not seen.

                Tools in LLMs (function calling)

                The AI asks. Your code does the work.

                An LLM cannot check live weather, do exact math or send an email by itself. With tools (also called function calling), your app tells the model which functions exist. The model replies "please call this function with these inputs". Your code runs it and sends the result back, and the model writes the final answer. Pick a question.

                Simplified. Real APIs (Gemini, Claude, OpenAI) describe tool inputs with JSON Schema. Results shown are example data.

                Model choices

                Small or large, local or cloud, open or closed

                There is no best model, only the best fit. Bigger models are usually smarter but slower and pricier. Local models run on your own machine, so data stays with you. Open-weight models can be downloaded and changed; closed models are only available through the company's API. Answer 4 questions.

                Why AI hallucinates

                It predicts words that sound right, not facts that are checked

                An LLM is trained to write the most likely next words. When a fact appeared many times in its training text, the likely words are also true. When it does not know, it still writes words that sound right, in the same confident tone. Watch the chance of each word, then switch to web search or RAG.

                Why it happens
                • It is trained to write fluent text, not to check it
                • There is no database of facts inside, only patterns stored as numbers
                • Rare facts (a small clinic, a new event) were seen rarely or never
                • Training often rewards giving an answer over saying "I don't know"
                • It knows nothing after its knowledge cutoff
                What reduces it
                • Ground answers in sources: web search or RAG
                • Require citations and show them to users
                • Tell it to say "I don't know" when the sources don't answer
                • Use a low temperature for factual tasks, on models that let you set it
                • Check important facts, and measure the rate (see Evaluating)

                      Collect real questions

                      Write the ideal answer

                      Add tricky cases

                      Compare before shipping
